1. Executive Summary

Rice in the Konkan coastal strip of Maharashtra is grown almost entirely as a rainfed kharif crop on lateritic, acidic, coastal-alluvial and coastal-saline (Kharland) soils, under very high (roughly 1,000­
3,500 mm) but erratic monsoon rainfall. The region's dedicated agricultural university, Dr. Balasaheb Sawant Konkan Krishi Vidyapeeth (DBSKKV), Dapoli, together with its constituent Regional
Agricultural Research Station at Karjat and the Kharland Research Station at Panvel, has released 25 improved rice varieties and 5 hybrids since 1972, several of which (Karjat2, Karjat3, Karjat7,
Karjat8, Karjat184, the Ratnagiri series, the Panvel series for saline soils, and the Sahyadri hybrids) have documented, sourced agronomic and disease/pest-resistance data. Official DBSKKV
JointAgresco recommendations give concrete fertilizer doses and sowing/establishment guidance for kharif rice in specific Konkan zones (not one universal number). A peer-reviewed DBSKKV agronomy
trial (Jagtap et al., 2017) gives sourced, meteorological-week-specific sowing-window results. Konkan-specific, sourced pest evidence exists (Hatwar et al., 2025, Vegetos journal) for stem borer, leaf
folder and case worm on Konkan farms, and blast is documented in the literature as agronomically important in Konkan specifically (IntechOpen, 2021) -- though a comparable-across-pests
incidence/severity ranking dataset for Konkan could not be located and must be explicitly flagged as unavailable. IMD district rainfall bulletins give real actual-vs-normal seasonal rainfall figures for the
five target districts. On the digital-tools side, the state's flagship advisory app has evolved rapidly (from generic MahaVISTAAR/MahaVISTAARAI to the BharatVISTAAR national platform and a 2026
MahaAgX data layer), all of which are cropgeneric and state/national in scope, not Konkanspecific or ricevarietyspecific decision engines -- this is the central research gap your proposed DSS
can credibly fill. Multiple existing ML "crop recommendation systems" exist for Maharashtra generally, but none identified in this search perform varietylevel, evidencecited, Konkanspecific rice
recommendation -- supporting (not proving, in the absence of an exhaustive systematic review) a genuine research gap for your project.

2. Verified Konkan Rice Varieties

2.1 Overview (official / university-level facts)

    DBSKKV, established 1972 (renamed 2001), has released 25 improved rice varieties and 5 hybrids as of the most recent institutional summary found. [Source: indiaagronet.com profile of
    DBSKKV; DBSKKV old.dbskkv.org Varieties page]
    The Regional Agricultural Research Station (RARS), Karjat, Raigad, under DBSKKV, is the lead rice-breeding station: it developed 11 straight (inbred) varieties and 4 hybrids since 1972 (10
    more were selected 1919­1970 before DBSKKV existed), and released India's first Maharashtra hybrid rice, Sahyadri, in 1998 (third rice hybrid released in India). [Source: The Better India, 2018,
    profile of RARS Karjat -- secondary journalism source, treat variety count as indicative, not official]
    Konkan accounts for roughly 29.65% of Maharashtra's total rice production against 23.70% of the area, i.e., Konkan rice productivity is above the state average. [Source: The Better India, 2018]
    A DBSKKV/ICAR journal article (Shinde et al., 2018, Advanced Agricultural Research & Technology Journal) states Konkan's average rough-rice productivity was 4.25 t/ha, higher than Western
    Maharashtra (3.5 t/ha) and Vidarbha (3.4 t/ha) in the reference year of that study. [Source: isasat.org, Shinde et al. 2018]

5. Sowing / Transplanting Window Analysis

5.1 Verified DBSKKV field-trial result (most specific evidence located)

Jagtap, D. N., Mahadkar, U. V., & Chavan, S. A. (2017). "Response of rice varieties to different sowing windows under Konkan conditions." Agricultural Science Digest, 37(1), 78­80.
- Conducted at Dept. of Agronomy, College of Agriculture, DBSKKV Dapoli, kharif 2015­16. - Design: split-plot, 3 replications; main plot = 3 sowing windows (23rd, 24th, 25th Meteorological Week);
sub-plot = 5 varieties (Karjat5, Palghar1, Jaya, Swarna, Karjat2). - Result: sowing in the 23rd Met. Week gave significantly higher grain yield (5,782 kg/ha) and straw yield (6,462
kg/ha) than the 24th and 25th Met. Weeks. - Among varieties, long-duration Swarna gave the highest yield, with Jaya statistically at par; both outperformed the other three varieties tested. -
Conclusion (verified, quoted in substance not verbatim): for Konkan kharif rice, the 23rd Meteorological Week is the recommended sowing window when growing Swarna or Jaya, for higher yield
and economic return.
This directly supports (and gives Konkan-specific experimental grounding to) the brief's reference to "22nd­25th MW."

5.2 Official transplanting-method recommendation (fertilizer/agronomy angle)
DBSKKV Joint Agresco2021 recommends, for rice cv. Karjat2 and Karjat7, either recommended-time transplanting or early transplanting (15 days after sowing) as the method to achieve
better performance and yield stability under Konkan's variable kharif rainfall. [Official DBSKKV recommendation]

5.3 General ICAR contingency-plan reference points (not Konkan-specific -- included for contrast only)

ICARCRRI's national rice contingency plan describes generic early/normal/late sowing-window fallback logic (e.g., early-medium duration varieties transplantable up to September first week under
delayed monsoon; direct-seeded short-duration varieties up to end-August under further delay). This is a national/Odisha-oriented contingency framework, not Konkan-specific, and must not
be presented as a Konkan recommendation -- it is included here only as a general methodological reference per the brief's instruction to consult ICAR sources; the actual Konkan-specific figure is
the DBSKKV 23rd/24th/25th MW study in §5.1.

8.4 Which weather variables could realistically feed the DSS (our inference, clearly labelled)

11.1 Research gap (defensible, evidence-based -- not an overclaim)

Based on the studies actually located in this session: 1. No system identified in this search performs rice-variety-level recommendation (as opposed to crop-choice recommendation) for
Konkan specifically. The closest match (Mahale et al. 2024) recommends which crop to grow per Maharashtra division, not which rice variety under given soil/salinity/sowing-window conditions. 2. No
system identified integrates DBSKKV's own official recommendations (Agresco doses, variety release data, sowing-window trial results) as a structured, citable evidence base --
existing ML systems train on generic Kaggle/IMD aggregate datasets, not on the region's own primary agricultural-research literature. 3. No system identified provides an explanation/evidence
layer (why a variety was selected, with a source) as opposed to a bare classification output.

Per the brief's explicit instruction, this is stated as "what is missing from existing systems," not as "no one has done this before." A truly exhaustive literature search (Scopus/Web of
Science with full-text access, which this session's search tool cannot fully replicate) might surface a closer Konkan-specific prior system; that possibility should be explicitly acknowledged in your
thesis/report's own literature-review chapter, with a note that this AI-assisted search is not a substitute for a full systematic database search via your university library's Scopus/Web of Science access.

12. Proposed DSS Architecture (built only on the verified findings above)

Modules, with rule-based vs. ML-driven vs. database-driven judgement grounded in what the evidence actually supports:

1. Farmer/Farm input module -- database-driven (district, soil type, field history).
2. Soil condition module -- database-driven/rule-based: encode the four Konkan soil types (lateritic, acidic, coastal alluvial, coastal saline/Kharland) as a lookup table; only the coastal-saline

    category currently has a sourced variety-mapping (Panvel1/2/3) -- the other three soil types need the DBSKKV soil-science gap in §4 filled before rule logic can be evidence-backed.
3. Weather module -- rule-based ingestion of IMD's public weekly/seasonal rainfall bulletins and alert levels (per §8.4); a full ML weather model is not justified by current data access unless a direct IMD

    data-sharing agreement is secured.
4. Rice variety database -- database-driven; populate directly from the verified table in §2.2, explicitly tagging each field as "official"/"experimental result"/"farmer-reported"/"not verified" per record,

    exactly as this report does.
5. Variety suitability engine -- rule-based, not ML, given the current evidence base: the strongest available Konkan-specific signal (Jagtap et al.'s meteorological-week trial; the Agresco

    Karjat2/Karjat7 rainfall-stability recommendation; the Panvel salinity association) are discrete, expert-validated rules, not a large enough labelled dataset for supervised ML to outperform a rule
    engine. This is our architectural recommendation, not a sourced fact -- flag it as such in your defense.
6. Pest/disease risk engine -- rule-based flagging only where evidence exists (e.g., flag salinity-zone fields for Panvel-line varieties; flag Konkan-wide for stem-borer/leaf-folder awareness per Hatwar
    et al. 2025) rather than a trained pest-prediction ML model, since a comparable-across-pests Konkan incidence dataset does not currently exist (§6.3) -- an ML pest-risk model would have no genuine
    Konkan ground truth to train on today.
7. Nutrient recommendation engine -- rule-based, directly encoding the two verified Agresco zone-specific dose tables (§9.1) plus an explicit farmer-practice gap flag (§9.2) rather than inventing a
      continuous response-curve model without underlying trial data.
 8. Evidence/knowledge base -- this report's own tables are a starting schema: every recommendation row should carry a source citation and an evidence-type tag (official/experimental/farmer-

      reported/inference).
 9. Recommendation/ranking layer -- rule-based weighted filtering (soil  salinity  sowing window  rainfall risk  candidate varieties), explicitly not a black-box ranker, so it can produce the

      explanation required in Part 12 of the brief.
10. Explanation layer -- template-based natural-language generation over the rule engine's decision trace (which rule fired, which evidence record it cites, and a plain confidence label -- "official

      recommendation" vs. "single experimental trial" vs. "farmer-reported only") -- this maps directly onto the evidence-quality tags used throughout this report.

  Where ML is justifiable given current evidence: none of the sub-components above currently have enough Konkan-specific labelled data to justify supervised ML over a transparent rule engine. If
  your team later obtains a genuine, sizeable Konkan multi-season dataset (e.g., via your planned §13 farmer/KVK interviews plus multi-year DBSKKV Agresco archives), a data-driven ranking layer could be
  layered on top of the rule engine's output as a secondary re-ranker -- but that is a future-work recommendation, not a current-evidence-based requirement.

RESEARCH INFERENCES

    The strongest available proxy for "which pests matter most in Konkan" is variety-level bred resistance (e.g., Ratnagiri7's resistance to stem borer/leaf folder/gall midge), since breeders would not
    target resistance to locally irrelevant pests -- this is our inference, not a direct incidence measurement.
    A rule-based (not ML) architecture is currently better justified than a trained ML model for most DSS sub-components, because the Konkan-specific evidence base located is composed of discrete
    expert trials/recommendations rather than a large labelled dataset.
    No system located in this search performs Konkan-specific, variety-level, evidence-cited rice recommendation -- supporting a genuine research gap, though not an exhaustive-search-confirmed one.
